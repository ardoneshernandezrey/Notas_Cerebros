Las conferencias que presento a continuación ante el público médico en general fueron impartidas a principios de este año, en el nuevo Instituto de Patología de la Universidad de Berlín, en presencia de una asamblea bastante numerosa de médicos, en su mayoría médicos que ejercen en la ciudad.
El objetivo principal de estas obras, ilustradas con la mayor cantidad posible de preparaciones microscópicas, era proporcionar una explicación clara y coherente de aquellos hechos sobre los que, según mis ideas, debe basarse ahora la teoría de la vida y a partir de los cuales también debe construirse la ciencia de la patología.
They were more particularly intended as an attempt to offer in a better arranged form than had hitherto becn done, a view of the cellular nature of all vital processes, both physiological and pathological, animal and vegetable, so as distinctly to set forth what even the people have long been dimly conscious of, namely, the unity of life¹ in all organized beings, in opposition to the one-sided humoral and neuristical (solidistic) tendencies which have been transmitted from the mythical days of 1
antiquity to our own times, and at the same time to contrast with the equally one-sided interpretations of a grossly mechanical and chemical bias-the more delicate mechanism and chemistry of the cell.

In consequence of the great advances that have been made in the details of science, it has been becoming continually more and more difficult to the majority of those who are engaged in practice, to obtain in the subjects treated on in these lectures that amount of personal experience which alone can guarantee a certain degree of accuracy of judgment. Day by day do those who are obliged to consume their best energies in the frcquently so toilsome and so exhausting routine of practice find it becoming less and less possible for them, not only to closely examine, but even to understand the more recent medical works. For even the language of medicine is gradually assuming another appearance; well-known processes to which the prevailing system had assigned a certain place and name in the circle of our thoughts, change with the dissolution of the system their position and their denomination. When a certain action is transferred from the nerves, blood, or vessels to the tissues, when a passive process is recognized to be an active one, an exudation to be a proliferation, then it becomes absolutely necessary to choose other cxpressions whereby these actions, processes, and products shall be designated; and in proportion as our knowledge of the more delicate modes, in which the processes of life are carried on, becomes more perfect, just in that proportion must the new denominations also be adapted to this more delicate ground-work of our knowledge.

It would not be easy for any one to attempt to carry out the necessary reform in medical opinion with more respect for tradition than I have made it my endeavour to ohserve. Still my own experience has taught me that even in this there is a certain limit. Too great respect is a real fault, for it favours confusion; a well-selected expression renders at once accessible para la comprensión de todos, lo que, sin ello, esfuerzos prolongados durante años solo podrían hacerse inteligible para unos pocos. Como ejemplos citaré los términos inflamación parenquimatosa, trombosis y embolia, leucemia e icorremia, tejido osteoide y mucoso, metamorfosis caseosa y amiloide, y sustitución de tejidos. No se pueden evitar los nuevos nombres cuando se trata de aportaciones reales al conocimiento experimental (empírico). Por otro lado, ya se me ha reprochado a menudo intentar rehabilitar puntos de vista anticuados en la ciencia moderna. Al respecto, creo poder decir con la conciencia tranquila que estoy tan poco inclinado a devolver a Galeno y Paracelso a la posición que antes ocupaban, como me da miedo reconocer abiertamente la verdad que puedan tener sus puntos de vista y observaciones. De hecho, encuentro no solo que los médicos de la antigüedad y la Edad Media no tenían en todos los casos sus sentidos encadenados por prejuicios tradicionales, sino más que esto, que entre el pueblo la excusa común se ha aferrado a ciertas verdades, a pesar de que la crítica de los eruditos las había declarado derrocadas. ¿Qué debería impedirme afirmar que la crítica de los eruditos no siempre ha resultado correcta, que el sistema no siempre ha sido la naturaleza, y que una interpretación falsa no menoscaba la veracidad del hecho? ¿Por qué no debería conservar buenas expresiones, o restaurarlas, aunque se les hayan atribuido ideas falsas? Mi experiencia me obliga a considerar el término fluxión (congestión activa-Wallung) 1 como preferible al de congestión; no puedo evitar admitir que la inflamación sea una forma definida en la que se manifiestan los procesos patológicos, aunque no puedo admitir que pretenda ser considerada como una entidad; y debo sostener, a pesar de las firmes contradeclaraciones de muchos investigadores, que el tubérculo es un gránulo miliar, y Epitelioma, una neoformación maligna heteroplásica (cancroide). Quizás hoy en día sea un mérito reconocer los derechos históricos, pues es realmente asombroso con qué ligereza aquellos mismos hombres que proclaman como un descubrimiento cada nimiedad con la que se topan, juzgan a sus predecesores. Yo defiendo mis propios derechos y, por lo tanto, también reconozco los derechos de los demás. Este es el principio por el que actúo en la vida, en la política y en la ciencia. Nos debemos a nosotros mismos defender nuestros derechos, pues es la única garantía para nuestro desarrollo individual y para nuestra influencia en la comunidad en general. Tal defensa no es un acto de vana ambición, ni implica la renuncia a los objetivos puramente científicos. Porque, si queremos servir a la ciencia, debemos extender sus límites, no solo en lo que respecta a nuestro propio conocimiento, sino también en la estima de los demás. Ahora bien, esta estima depende en gran medida del reconocimiento otorgado a nuestros derechos, de la confianza depositada en nuestras investigaciones por los demás; Y esta es la razón por la que defiendo mis derechos.
In a science so directly practical as that of medicine, and at a time when such a rapid accumulation of facts is taking place, as there is in ours, we are doubly bound to render our knowledge accessible to the whole body of our professional brethren. We would have reform, and not revolution: we would preserve the old, and add the new. But our contemporaries have a confused idea of the results of our activity. For only too much it is apt to appear as though nought but a confused and motley mass of old and new would thereby be obtained; and the necessity of combating rather the false or exclusive doctrines of the more modern, than those of the older writers, produces the impression that our endeavours savour more of revolution than reformation. It is, no doubt, much more agreeable to confine oncself to the investigation and simple publication of what one discovers, and to leave to others to "take it to market" but experience teaches us that this is ехtremely dangerous, and in the end only turns out to the advantage of those who have the least tenderness of conscience. Let us undertake, therefore, every one of us to fulfil the duties both of an observer and of an instructor. The lectures, which I here publish with the view of accomplishing this double purpose, have found such very patient auditors, that they may perhaps venture to hope for indulgent readers likewise. How greatly they stand in need of indulgence, I myself feel very strongly. Every kind of lecture can only satisfy the actual hearers ; and especially when it is chiefly intended to serve as an explanation of drawings on a board, and microscopical preparations, it must necessarily appear heterogeneous and defective to the reader. When the intention is to give a concise view of a comprehensive subject, it necessarily becomes impossible to bring forward all the arguments that could be advanced, and to support them by the requisite quotations. In lectures such as these too the personal views of the lecturer may seem to be brought forward with undue exclusiveness, but, as it is his business to give a clear exposition of the actual state of the science of which he treats, he is obliged to define with precision the principles, the correctness of which he has proved by his own experience. I trust therefore that what I offer may not be taken for more than it is intended to be. Those, who have found leisure cnough to keep up their knowledge by reading the current medical literature, will find but little that is new in these lectures. The rest will not, by reading them, be spared the trouble of being obliged to study the subjects, which are here only briefly touched upon, more closely in the special histological, physiological and pathological works.
