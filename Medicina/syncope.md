Syncope is a common clinical problem caused by transient cerebral hypoperfusion
and encompasses reflex, orthostatic, and cardiac mechanisms. Accurate diagnosis
relies on careful history taking and physical examination, as well as targeted testing
guided by risk stratification. Management of syncope is determined on the
basis of the underlying mechanism and focuses on reducing recurrence and injury
risk. In older patients, syncope may manifest as unexplained falls. Early identification
of high-risk features is critical to prevent adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

A 75-year-old retired man who lives alone presents to the emergency department
with a zygomatic fracture after an unexplained fall. He has a history of hypertension,
hyperlipidemia, and depression, for which he is receiving treatment. The incident
occurred in the early afternoon while he was walking his dog. It was associated with
urinary incontinence, and the patient has no recollection of the event. There were no
witnesses. Over the preceding 6 months, he reports having had one similar fall and
two episodes of transient loss of consciousness. These episodes occurred after he
stood up after eating a meal or sitting for a prolonged period or during nocturnal
urination. All events were unwitnessed. How should this case be managed?

The Clinical Problem
yncope, or a fainting episode, is defined as a transient loss of
consciousness accompanied by an inability to maintain postural tone, resulting from temporary cerebral hypoperfusion, which is followed by rapid
and spontaneous recovery. Episodes are typically brief, usually lasting less than
1 minute.1
 Causes of syncope are classified as reflex syncope, cardiac syncope, and
syncope due to orthostatic hypotension.
Syncope and falls frequently overlap, particularly in older adults, making the
distinction between the two events clinically challenging.2
 The incidence of unexplained falls increases with age, rising from approximately 4% per year among
adults 50 to 60 years of age to approximately 8% per year among adults 70 years
of age or older, as compared an incidence of syncope of 4.4% per year across
similar age groups. Approximately one in five events results in an injury leading
to hospital evaluation.2
 Falls account for approximately 15% of emergency department visits among older adults,3
 and 20 to 30% of such falls may be unexplained,
potentially reflecting underlying syncope.

Syncope is common, affecting one third to
one half of the general population during their
lifetime. Syncope is reported more frequently in
women than in men, with prevalence estimates
of 22% and 15%, respectively.4
 The cumulative
incidence increases markedly with age, ranging
from approximately 5% among women 20 to 29
years of age to nearly 50% among those 80 years
of age or older, with the lifetime cumulative incidence among women being nearly twice that
observed among men.2,5 Recurrent syncope occurs in up to 20% of affected persons and is
associated with greater risks of death (hazard
ratio, 1.87; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.26 to
2.77) and major adverse cardiovascular events
(hazard ratio, 2.69; 95% CI, 2.02 to 3.59) than
nonrecurrent syncope over a follow-up period of
24 months

Reflex syncope results from dysregulation of
the autonomic nervous system leading to vasodilation, bradycardia, or both. It includes vasovagal syncope, situational syncope, and carotid sinus
syndrome.6
 Reflex syncope accounts for more
than 90% of cases among younger adults but
only approximately 40% of cases among older
adults.5-7 Vasovagal syncope is the most common
type of syncope and represents a reflex, neurally
mediated faint that results from bradycardia
(vagal excess), hypotension (withdrawal of sympathetic innervation), or most commonly, a combination of the two. Although vasovagal syncope
generally carries a benign prognosis, injuries
occur in more than 30% of episodes, with major
injuries reported in approximately 15%. Older
age and recurrent syncope or falls increase the
likelihood of injury, as does the lack of a recognizable prodrome, which would allow patients to
take preventive action (e.g., lying down) before
the occurrence of syncope.8
 Situational syncope
is reflex syncope due to an action or situation
such as emotional stress, micturition, swallowing, or coughing. Carotid sinus syndrome occurs
almost exclusively in older adults and accounts
for 9 to 17% of syncopal events and up to 30%
of otherwise-unexplained falls.

Orthostatic hypotension also causes syncope
and falls.10 The prevalence of orthostatic hypotension increases with age, affecting less than
5% of persons younger than 50 years of age,
more than 20% of persons older than 70 years
of age, and up to 50 to 68% of older adults living
in institutional settings.11 Cardiac syncope is rare in younger adults, accounting for up to 5%
of events, but the incidence increases with age,
reaching 30% of syncopal events among older
patients and leading to higher morbidity and
mortality.7
 In older adults, multiple potential contributing mechanisms may coexist in up to one
third of cases — a condition referred to as complex syncope.9,10
As diagnostic strategies and management
guidelines have evolved over the past three decades, the percentage of syncopal events that are
unexplained has decreased substantially (Fig. 1),
declining from approximately 37% in early studies12 to less than 10% in more recent cohorts in
which structured diagnostic pathways and prolonged rhythm monitoring were used.13 Consequently, tailored interventions are advocated and
range from conservative management of triggers and deprescribing in hypotensive syncope
to cardiac pacing and cardioneural ablation for
problematic bradyarrhythmias or to the use of
implantable cardiac defibrillators for serious ventricular arrhythmias.
